NE NASI MON / 根無し者

Food knowledge travels with people.
It changes, adapts and takes root again.

Nenashimono (根無し者) literally means someone without roots.

For me, being rootless does not mean being without connection. I think about the knowledge and practices we carry with us as we move — ways of cooking, preserving and fermenting; tastes and memories; ways of looking at an ingredient; even a sourdough starter.

When these practices arrive somewhere new, they do not remain unchanged. They encounter different ingredients, climates, cultures and people who carry their own knowledge. We observe, learn and adapt. Something is carried forward, something changes, and something new can grow from the encounter.

My own practice has developed this way. Its foundation is in Japanese home cooking and the ways ingredients are prepared, preserved and transformed. Moving to Finland led me deeper into fermentation, foraging and working with what grows around me. Working in other places has taught me to arrive with what I know, learn from what is already there, and let the encounter change what I make.

Nenasimon is the name I give to this evolving practice.

RESIDENCY

Halkidiki & Thessaloniki, Greece

2024

Learning a place through its ingredients, people and landscape.

I spent two and a half weeks in Northern Greece exploring the ingredients and food practices of Halkidiki while developing my own menus.

I cooked alongside Greek chefs and learned by watching, asking and doing. I worked with local rice and sardines, herbs growing around the property, coastal plants, mulberry leaves, flowers, semolina and other ingredients I encountered there.

At the same time, I brought my own practices with me. My sourdough starter travelled with me and was adapted to Greek semolina flour, rosemary and sesame. Local herbs took the place of Japanese herbs I might normally have reached for. Flowers gathered nearby became syrups for desserts.

Finding mulberry trees around the property also led me to research their history of use in Japan. I experimented with making tea from the leaves as well as using them in cooking.

It wasn't about making Japanese food in Greece, or trying to become a Greek cook. It was about bringing what I know into conversation with what was already there.

The residency concluded with a brunch in Halkidiki and a dinner event at Kitchen Suite in Thessaloniki.

FOOD

My cooking begins with ingredients and the knowledge around them.

My foundation is Japanese home cooking — not only its dishes, but the ways ingredients are observed, prepared, preserved and transformed.

Since moving to Finland, my study of Japanese fermentation has expanded into sourdough, lacto-fermentation, kombucha, foraging and fermentation practices from elsewhere.

What fascinates me is how fermentation can be both universal and intensely local: similar knowledge and processes take different forms through the ingredients, climate and food culture of a place.

My miso workshops are one way I explore this. Rather than treating miso as a fixed recipe made only from soybeans and rice koji, I use the knowledge behind the process as a starting point for experimenting with locally available legumes and grains.

The practice travels. The ingredients belong to the place.

SAKE

Sake through food, hospitality and education.

I am a certified Kikizakeshi (SSI sake sommelier), working with restaurants, hospitality professionals and consumers to make sake easier to understand, serve, pair and talk about.

My background as a cook is an important part of this work. I approach sake not in isolation, but in relation to food — including Nordic and European cuisines as well as Japanese food — and to the context in which people actually encounter and drink it.

I am also fascinated by sake makers working outside Japan: people who carry brewing knowledge into another landscape, or learn the practice from outside Japanese culture and reinterpret it through local ingredients, food cultures and sensibilities.

For restaurants & hospitality

Sake List & Selection

Support in building or developing a sake list — selecting styles, creating a balanced range, organising the list and making it understandable to guests.

Food & Sake Pairing

Pairing concepts for existing menus, tasting menus and special events, including the language and story used to communicate each pairing.

Staff Training

Practical training covering sake fundamentals, styles, tasting, storage and service, food pairing, and how to confidently explain and recommend sake to customers.

Guest Sake Sommelier

Guest shifts and collaborative events with restaurants — presenting sake directly to guests, developing pairings with the kitchen, or creating a sake-focused dinner or pop-up together.

For people

Guided Tastings

From introductions for first-time sake drinkers to thematic tastings exploring styles, regions, fermentation and food.

Sake & Food Events

Pairing dinners, pop-ups and collaborative events with chefs, restaurants, importers and producers.

RESIDENCIES & COLLABORATIONS

I am interested in what becomes possible when there is enough time to learn from a place.

I am open to residency programmes and collaborative projects that allow me to spend time with local ingredients, landscapes and food practices — particularly projects where I can learn alongside cooks, farmers, producers, foragers and other people carrying local knowledge.

I bring my own practices with me, but I am not interested in simply placing them somewhere else unchanged.

I want to learn what is already there, and see what can grow from the encounter.

ONGOING PROJECT

Zestii Kitchens

I co-founded Zestii Kitchens in Finland as a social enterprise exploring another side of the same questions that inform my personal practice: whose food knowledge becomes visible, how people can build livelihoods from the knowledge they already carry, and what kind of shared infrastructure makes that possible.

Through shared kitchen infrastructure, café operations, pop-ups, workshops and catering, Zestii creates ways for independent food practitioners to test, develop and offer their work without having to build the entire infrastructure alone.

ABOUT

Minaë Tani-LaFleur

Minaë Tani-LaFleur is a Helsinki-based food practitioner and certified Kikizakeshi (SSI sake sommelier).

Her practice moves between cooking, fermentation, sake, education and community-based food projects. Rooted in Japanese home cooking and shaped by living and working across different places, her work explores how food knowledge travels, adapts and encounters local ingredients and practices.

She is co-founder of Zestii Kitchens, a social enterprise developing shared infrastructure and opportunities for small food entrepreneurs in Finland, and is active within the Slow Food movement.

Alongside her food and sake practice, she continues to study Japanese tea through the Urasenke tradition.
